# Evidence standards

## Record the kind of statement

Use the narrowest appropriate category:

- **Documented fact:** directly supported by a traceable primary or authoritative source.
- **Reported claim:** a source reports it, but the project has not independently established it.
- **Interpretation:** an explanation or synthesis drawn from evidence.
- **Hypothesis:** plausible but not yet adequately tested.
- **Unknown / evidence gap:** information is missing, conflicting, inaccessible, or too weak to support a conclusion.

## Source quality and context

Record author or issuing organisation, title, publication date, URL or stable location, language, geographic scope, source type, access date where useful, and relevant limitations. Check whether a source is current enough for the claim and whether it actually addresses the population or island in question.

Prefer primary documents for what an organisation formally published or decided; use independent reporting, research, and local knowledge to examine context and effects. A primary source is not automatically impartial or complete.

## Claims and citations

- Make each claim narrow enough to verify.
- Link each claim to the specific supporting source.
- Note contrary evidence and meaningful uncertainty.
- Distinguish a source's words from a contributor's summary.
- Do not infer prevalence from isolated cases.
- Do not infer causation from correlation alone.
- Do not generalise from one island, institution, or community to all of Cabo Verde.
- Do not turn absence of accessible evidence into proof that something never happened.

## Intervention evidence

Describe the setting, participants, comparison or baseline if available, outcomes measured, timeframe, cost, delivery conditions, limitations, adverse effects, and transferability. Label an intervention as proposed, in testing, supported in a specified context, or not supported by current evidence. Avoid the unqualified label “proven” unless the evidence warrants it and the scope is stated.

## Behaviour, influence, and religion

Assess specific behaviours and measurable consequences. Participation in religion, media, online communities, or political activity is not by itself evidence of addiction or harm. Where concern exists, look for observable indicators such as coercion, deception, financial exploitation, isolation, interference with healthcare, inability to refuse, or barriers to exit. Apply comparable standards across sectors and explain the context.

## Ethics and safety

Do not publish sensitive personal data or expose people to retaliation. Seek informed consent for testimony, explain intended use and public visibility, and offer a safe way to withdraw where feasible. Do not conduct experiments that deny essential services or expose participants to unreasonable risk.
