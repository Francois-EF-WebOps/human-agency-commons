# Human agency: working framework

## Working definition

Human agency is a person's practical ability to understand their situation, form or revise intentions, make meaningful choices, act, and influence what happens next. Agency depends both on personal capacities and on the material, social, legal, and informational conditions around a person.

This is a working project definition, not a claim that one philosophical or scientific definition is universally accepted.

## Questions to ask

1. What choices are genuinely available—not merely advertised?
2. What resources, rights, time, skills, information, or support are needed to act?
3. Who sets the available options and incentives?
4. Can a person ask questions, disagree, decline, pause, or leave without punishment?
5. Are choices informed, accessible, and proportionate to the risks?
6. Who benefits, who bears costs, and who is excluded?
7. What evidence would show that the person's practical freedom improved?

## Agency is not isolated individual responsibility

A person may understand a problem and still lack money, transport, safe housing, time, water, care support, legal protection, or accessible services. A useful response addresses these constraints instead of blaming the person for not choosing an unavailable option.

## Practical safeguards

- Informed, voluntary participation
- Clear and truthful information
- Realistic alternatives
- Ability to refuse or exit
- Privacy and data minimisation
- Accessible language and formats
- Transparent costs and trade-offs
- A route to challenge decisions
- Monitoring for unequal effects and unintended harm

## Possible indicators

Choose indicators with affected people before a test. Depending on the context, these may include the number of usable options, out-of-pocket cost, time required, access by island or income group, ability to refuse without penalty, informed understanding, service reliability, self-reported control, and adverse effects. No single indicator fully measures agency.
