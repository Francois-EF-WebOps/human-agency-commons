# Solution stories and implementation artefacts

**Purpose:** A curated, evidence-linked library of real interventions from around the world, presented as journeys from challenge to implementation, observed results, user feedback, and lessons for responsible adaptation.

This is not a gallery of success stories. Include partial successes, failed or discontinued approaches, trade-offs, contested results, and maintenance problems. The aim is to help readers understand what was actually done, how it worked in context, what changed, what people said, and what can or cannot transfer elsewhere.

## How to read each story

Every story must keep these categories separate:

- **Need and baseline:** the problem, who experienced it, where and when, and how it was measured.
- **Expected outcomes:** what implementers said they intended to achieve before or during delivery.
- **Implementation:** the actual policy, service, process, infrastructure, application, or community practice; operators, partners, costs, and timeline where documented.
- **Reported results:** measured or reported changes, with dates, denominators and attribution caveats.
- **Feedback and experience:** direct user/community feedback or systematically collected feedback, with provenance. If no reliable feedback is found, say so; never invent quotes or imply official results are user sentiment.
- **What went wrong / remained unresolved:** exclusions, outages, delays, cost, governance, sustainability and unintended consequences.
- **Evidence quality:** primary reports, independent evaluation, local reporting, interviews, source conflicts and gaps.
- **Transferability:** context required, local validation needed, and reasons not to copy blindly.

## Global starting points

These are **research leads based on publicly available institutional reporting**, not completed independent evaluations. The linked source summaries establish reported results, but do not necessarily provide independent user feedback or causal proof. Follow source links to original project documents before treating the cases as fully validated.

### 1. Philippines — PhilSys national identification and connected services

**Challenge and turning point.** A national identification system was intended to make identity proof more broadly accessible and support access to financial and public services. The implementation story includes legal foundations, registration access, privacy-by-design, and linking identity registration with the opportunity to open bank accounts.

**What was implemented.** National digital identity registration and issuance, with free bank-account opening offered through registration centres for eligible poor applicants, according to World Bank reporting.

**Reported results.** The World Bank's 2023 results summary reported 80 million registrations (about 90% of the eligible population at that time), most registrants having received an ID, and 8 million new bank accounts opened through the linked programme. A separate 2026 World Bank programme summary reports 80 million enrolled, 60 million IDs issued, 8 million new bank accounts, benefit processing reduced from four days to one, and business-permit processing reduced by 80%. These are dated institutional reports; definitions, programme periods and attribution should be checked in source documents before combining the figures.

**Feedback status.** The summaries linked here do not provide a representative direct account of users' experiences. Seek registration-access studies, privacy/exclusion complaints, independent evaluation, and feedback from people who could not register or use the ID.

**Implementation lesson to investigate.** A foundational identity system may unlock services, but access, data protection, correction/appeal routes, exclusion risks and non-digital alternatives are part of the outcome—not optional add-ons.

Sources:
- World Bank, *Creating Digital Public Infrastructure for Empowerment, Inclusion, and Resilience* (2023): https://www.worldbank.org/en/results/2023/10/12/creating-digital-public-infrastructure-for-empowerment-inclusion-and-resilience
- World Bank Group, *Global Digital Public Infrastructure Program: From Foundations to Scale* (2026): https://www.worldbank.org/en/results/2026/05/06/global-digital-public-infrastructure-program

**Status:** Initial institutional source lead; full source verification, independent evaluation and user feedback research pending.

### 2. Tanzania — rural water and sanitation at scale

**Challenge and turning point.** Expanding infrastructure alone does not guarantee reliable water and sanitation. The programme narrative describes a shift toward functioning water points, local organisations, operating capacity, monitoring and women's participation in water governance.

**What was implemented.** A combination of infrastructure investment, institutional strengthening, community-based water supply organisations, operational-maintenance capacity, sector data systems and behaviour-change work.

**Reported results.** A World Bank Group FY26 results narrative reports that by completion more than 11.2 million people had gained access to improved water sources and 12.3 million to improved sanitation; over 83,000 water points were functioning sustainably; and more than 12,500 villages were served by organisations with strengthened operations-and-maintenance capacity. The narrative also describes time saved collecting water and improved school and health-facility conditions. These are programme-reported results; evaluation methods and definitions must be checked in the underlying documentation.

**Feedback status.** The source describes experiences in Kiperesa village, including residents who previously walked long distances to fetch water. This is a reported case illustration, not a representative survey of every beneficiary. Locate underlying evaluation and locally recorded feedback before quoting or generalising.

**What remained unresolved.** The same source notes financial sustainability constraints, unpredictable revenues and tariffs that may not cover service costs, as well as slower progress in some monitoring and institutional work.

**Implementation lesson to investigate.** Build the operating and maintenance system alongside the physical asset; measure functioning service and affordability, not only construction or initial access.

Source:
- World Bank Group Scorecard, *Inclusive and Equitable Water and Sanitation Services — FY26 results narrative*: https://scorecard.worldbank.org/en/narratives/inclusive-and-equitable-water-and-sanitation-services/results-narrative/fy26

**Status:** Initial institutional source lead; verify the exact programme, report period, definitions and independent evaluation.

### 3. Morocco and Rio de Janeiro, Brazil — transport designed around women's safety and access

**Challenge and turning point.** Standard transport access measures can conceal different barriers faced by women, including safety and the ability to reach essential support. These examples ask whether targeted design and transport assistance can improve practical mobility.

**What was implemented.** The World Bank's She Drives Change case-study explorer describes a Morocco urban transport programme using female-friendly infrastructure, targeted services and inclusive policies. It also describes Rio de Janeiro's Cartão Move Mulher programme, providing free public transport to women experiencing domestic violence to help them access essential support services.

**Reported results.** The case-study explorer reports 100,000 daily users of the Morocco programme, 45% women, and more than 4,000 women using Rio's targeted free-transport programme. These figures are summaries and need confirmation against the individual case studies, their timeframes, eligibility criteria and outcome measures.

**Feedback status.** Direct participant feedback, safety outcomes, denied applications, geographic reach and long-term effects are not established by the summary alone. For the Rio case in particular, protect survivor privacy and do not seek identifiable testimony without a safe, voluntary, ethical process.

**Implementation lesson to investigate.** Treat mobility as access to opportunities and services, not merely passenger numbers. Assess safety, eligibility, dignity, privacy, service availability and whether people can use the programme without stigma.

Source:
- World Bank, *She Drives Change — Case Study Explorer*: https://shedriveschange.worldbank.org/case-study-explorer

**Status:** Discovery lead; retrieve and review individual case-study records before using the results as established evidence.

### 4. Ghana — mobile-based beneficiary feedback for public services

**Challenge and turning point.** Service providers need to understand the experience of people using services, not only monitor internal performance. Feedback systems can help identify problems, but collecting feedback does not itself prove that institutions respond effectively.

**What was implemented.** A World Bank project supported a feasibility study of mobile-based beneficiary feedback. The project summary says it collected real-time feedback and made recommendations for adjustments to feedback systems; findings were being implemented.

**Reported results.** The public summary establishes feasibility work, feedback collection and recommendations, but does not give enough information to claim improved service outcomes.

**Feedback status.** The collected beneficiary feedback is the central evidence to retrieve. Find out who could respond, who was excluded, how reports were verified, whether complaints were resolved, and whether participants saw changes.

**Implementation lesson to investigate.** Trace the complete feedback loop: submission → triage → response → resolution → communication back to the person. Count resolved issues and user trust, not only incoming messages.

Source:
- World Bank, *GovTech Projects* (Ghana: Mobile-Based Beneficiary Feedback Feasibility Project): https://www.worldbank.org/en/programs/govtech/projects

**Status:** Early evidence lead; outcome and feedback-loop verification pending.

## Editorial rule: make the journey visible

For each selected case, tell the story through sourced moments rather than an invented hero narrative:

1. **The pressure:** the original condition and its documented human consequences.
2. **The discovery:** the evidence or community feedback that changed the understanding of the problem.
3. **The choice:** the alternatives considered and why this approach was selected.
4. **The work:** the people, tools, institutions, money, compromises and setbacks involved.
5. **The first proof:** what was delivered and what early evidence showed.
6. **The lived result:** what changed for users, including people left out or harmed.
7. **The reality check:** costs, maintenance, failure, trade-offs and uncertainty.
8. **The next chapter:** what changed after evaluation and what a different place would need to validate.

Do not fabricate dialogue, dates, emotional states, causal links or community consensus. If the record is silent, make the gap visible. Use photographs, screenshots, forms, service maps, source documents, code, policy records, feedback summaries and evaluation tables only with traceable origins and suitable reuse permissions.
